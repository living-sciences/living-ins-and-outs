## Summary

This paper reassesses whether U.S. unemployment fluctuations are driven by how easily workers find jobs (the "outs") or by how often they lose them (the "ins"). Its central move is a transparent measurement device: under two strong assumptions (no flows in or out of the labor force, and identical workers), the job-finding probability and the employment-exit probability can be recovered from three publicly available aggregate series, unemployment, employment, and short-term unemployment, in a continuous-time model that corrects for time aggregation within the month. From these measures the paper finds that fluctuations in the job-finding probability account for about three-quarters of unemployment-rate volatility since 1948, rising to roughly 90 percent after 1987, while the exit probability is comparatively acyclic and has declined secularly since the early 1980s. The paper then relaxes each assumption in turn: a three-state (employment/unemployment/inactivity) gross-flows analysis, and a heterogeneity analysis across seven observable dimensions. Both are shown to leave the qualitative conclusion intact, which is the paper's rhetorical strategy: strong assumptions deliver strong results, so the results are stress-tested against weaker ones. The contribution matters because it overturned a then-dominant view, built on job-destruction evidence, that recessions are primarily episodes of elevated separations, and it redirected labor-market modeling toward the job-finding margin. The reasoning is careful and the headline measurement is robust: the two-state core reproduces cleanly from public data and, per completed follow-up work, continues to hold in data through 2026. The main limitations are not conceptual but evidentiary and reproductive. The headline shares are read off separate regressions rather than an exact variance partition; the finding/exit split is sensitive to a nonstandard smoothing choice; and the microdata-dependent sections (heterogeneity, prime-age men, the gross-flow construction) cannot be rerun from the released package. These qualify the precision of specific numbers, not the paper's thesis.

## Strengths

- **Two-state identity recovers job finding and exit from three public series.** The core construction reproduces almost exactly from publicly available aggregate data: the 1948-2010 decomposition comes out 0.763/0.245 against the paper's 0.77/0.24, mean job-finding probability 0.44, mean exit probability 3.4 percentage points, and the 0.98 correlation between the steady-state approximation and next month's unemployment rate. This is the paper's backbone and it holds.
- **The time-aggregation correction is real and quantified.** Detrending both the eq. (5) exit probability and the naive eq. (6) version reproduces the reported standard deviations (0.077 vs 0.109 against the paper's 0.07 vs 0.11), confirming that ignoring within-month finding-and-losing inflates the measured cyclicality of exits, which is one of the paper's four stated contributions.
- **Independent cross-check from gross flows.** The two-state finding and exit probabilities track the three-state UE and EU transition probabilities closely despite being built from different data (reproduced correlations 0.95 and 0.85, level gaps of ~32 and ~59 log points, near the paper's 0.96/31 and 0.83/56), and the three-state steady-state measure correlates 0.99 with next month's unemployment rate.
- **The secular decline in the exit probability is robustly present.** The reproduced exit-probability series falls from ~0.044 in the early 1980s to ~0.023 by the late 2000s, with the three recession spikes appearing only as modest bumps against the downtrend, matching the paper's characterization.

## Weaknesses

- **The microdata-dependent sections cannot be reproduced from the released materials.** The Section 4 heterogeneity analysis, the basis for rejecting the heterogeneity hypothesis, together with the prime-age-men results (Table 1 column 5, Figure 3) and the from-scratch gross-flow construction, all require CPS public-use microdata (~42 GB) and processing scripts that are not in the package. These support a stated contribution and a claim (that composition does not drive the job-finding decline) that a reader currently cannot verify.
- **The post-1987 exit share is sensitive to series construction.** Our reconstructed series initially gave a 1987-2010 split of 0.858/0.144 against the paper's 0.90/0.10, but the follow-up study that applied the paper's own Appendix A adjustment (the 1.1 factor of footnote 38) reproduced the published split to within a point (0.898/0.107). The deviation traces to how the reconstruction handles the post-1994 CPS redesign, not to the paper; stating the construction explicitly next to the decomposition would make that robustness visible.
- **The headline shares rest on approximate regressions, not an exact partition.** The finding and exit contributions are separate regression coefficients that happen to sum near one (footnote 11 reports 0.99-1.01; the three-state analog 1.01-1.03). The reproduction shows the exit contribution drifting outward for the smaller subsample values while the job-finding side stays close, so the exact percentages carry more construction-dependence than their round-number presentation suggests.
- **The three-state Table 2 reproduces in structure but not cell-by-cell, and its recent-decades claims are unverifiable as shipped.** The dominant UE row matches, but several smaller cells sit 5-30 percent off (one beyond 30 percent), and because the released transition-rate series end in 2004Q4, the "three times as volatile" statement for the last two decades and the noted 2009-2010 divergence in Figure 4 fall outside the reproducible window.

## Reproducibility & code

The replication reconstructed and executed the pipeline against the released repository. The weighted replication score is 0.63 (25 of 27 claims counted; 2 supporting claims fell outside the replication plan's scope), reflecting a cleanly reproducing two-state core alongside a set of microdata-dependent claims that could not be attempted.

- **Two-state core reproduces, but only after a manual tail-splice.** All headline two-state values regenerate closely. Reaching the 1948-2010 endpoint required adding the published short-term-unemployment level past January 2005, because the shipped microdata-share window ends there; the default released code stops the short-term series at 2005. This is a completeness gap in the released programs, not a challenge to the result.
- **Table 2 cells drift on the shipped-vintage sample.** Several Table 2 cells miss by 5-30 percent on the shipped 1967-2004 data vintage while preserving the qualitative structure and all signs; the post-1987 two-state split reproduces once the paper's own series adjustment is applied (see Weaknesses).
- **Figure 2 is not regenerated by the shipped code.** The plotting routine emits a scatter of the finding and exit probabilities against the unemployment rate rather than the published two-panel hypothetical-unemployment-rate time series. The series needed for the intended figure do reproduce.
- **Whole sections could not be attempted.** Reason-for-unemployment and demographic decompositions (the 30.7% vs 46.9% job-finding rates, the -0.79 correlation, F-comp/F-real, Figure 9), prime-age men, the short-term-unemployment rotation-group shares, the Bleakley gross-flow cross-check, and several Section 5 magnitudes all require microdata or external sources not shipped. The upstream construction of the instantaneous transition rates likewise needs the ~42 GB microdata plus Stata, so only the already-computed rate series (ending 2004Q4) could be checked.

## Follow-up studies

Two completed follow-up studies extend the analysis and largely vindicate it. The first re-estimates the decomposition on data through mid-2026: the anchor 1948-2010 job-finding share reproduces (0.769 against the paper's 0.77), and the post-1987 job-finding share holds and even strengthens 16 years past the paper's sample (0.892 for 1987-2026 excluding 2020, against the paper's 0.90). The one exception is the 2020 COVID layoff shock: including it, the 2010-2026 exit contribution jumps to 0.38 (the largest employment-exit contribution in the study) and separations "win" for four quarters, the one episode the constant-share framework cannot generate. The second study formalizes this as a rare "layoff regime" active in only about 0.2 percent of months (March-April 2020), during which separations drive 81 percent of the unemployment surge; it also documents that flows have slowed (adjustment speed 0.47 to 0.34), making unemployment about 53 percent more persistent, and that the exit probability's secular decline continued (mean 0.033 for 1948-2010 to 0.021 for 2010-2026). These results sharpen the paper's "exits are quantitatively irrelevant in recent decades" claim: it holds in normal times but not in an extreme layoff episode, which bears directly on the overstated "Throughout the time period" framing in the conclusion.

## Recommended Changes

**Essential**

- Release the microdata-processing scripts (or a documented build plus the derived group-level series) for the Section 4 reason-for-unemployment and demographic decompositions, the prime-age-men series (Table 1 column 5, Figure 3), and the gross-flow transition-rate construction, so the heterogeneity-hypothesis rejection and the three-state results can be independently verified.
- Include the post-2005 short-term-unemployment share in the released package so the full 1948-2010 two-state series regenerate without a manual tail-splice.
- Ship the plotting routine that reproduces Figure 2 as the published hypothetical-unemployment-rate time series rather than the current scatter.

**Suggested**

- Report the 1987-2010 decomposition with both the job-finding and exit contributions and the column sum, and state that the shares are separate regression coefficients rather than an exact partition (addresses the post-1987 split and the approximate-decomposition weakness).
- Report the Table 1 and Table 2 decompositions under the standard HP smoothing parameter 1600 alongside 10^5, so the sensitivity of the finding/exit split to the filter is visible.
- Extend the released three-state transition series through 2010 so the "three times as volatile" statement and the Figure 4 end-of-sample divergence become reproducible.
- Report a margin-error-corrected version of the Table 2 decomposition, or reference the specific corrected estimates, given that the paper concedes it cannot address gross-flows measurement error.
- Fix the small text slips: "short-term employment" should read "short-term unemployment" in the introduction; Figure 9's "changes in the unemployment rate" should read "job finding probability"; the eigenvector matrix is described as a "matrix of eigenvalues"; and the Caballero and Hammour (2005) reference year and journal volume are inconsistent.

## Future Directions

- **A regime-switching decomposition for rare separation-driven surges.** The follow-up work shows the constant-share framework cannot represent an episode like 2020, where separations drove 81 percent of the unemployment surge. Extending the measurement to allow a low-probability layoff regime would let the same accounting cover both normal times and mass-layoff shocks.
- **Consequences of slowing flows for the job-finding-centric agenda.** The documented fall in the adjustment speed (0.47 to 0.34) and the resulting ~53 percent rise in unemployment persistence raise the question of whether a lower job-finding rate now propagates shocks differently than in the paper's sample, which is worth revisiting within the search-model framework the paper motivates.
- **The exit-rate lead over job finding.** The paper flags Fujita and Ramey's finding that the exit rate leads the job-finding rate by about a quarter as meriting further exploration; a structural treatment could distinguish whether this temporal precedence reflects a causal channel or the within-model dynamics of Mortensen-Pissarides-style models.
